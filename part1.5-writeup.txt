Part 1.5 compression across morphological types

part1.5-analysis.py trains both tokenizers on the concatenated cleaned training text of all seven languages, the same corpus as the BPE run. BPE is trained once to 50,000 merges; a smaller vocabulary is the prefix of that merge list, which is the same model training would have stopped at. Unigram is trained separately at each size with the same SentencePiece settings as part 1.4. The compression ratio is tokens divided by UTF-8 bytes on each language's cleaned dev.txt. Lower means one token covers more of the text. The curves are in artifacts/part1/analysis/compression.png, and the numbers are in compression_ratios.tsv.

BPE at 50,000, tokens per byte: Russian 0.162, Arabic 0.166, Vietnamese 0.209, Mandarin 0.241, Finnish 0.251, Hungarian 0.255, German 0.263. That order is already there at 5,000 (Russian 0.270, German 0.421) and the lines fall smoothly as the vocabulary grows. From 5,000 to 50,000 every language sheds about 35 to 40 percent of its tokens. The height of a curve follows how many bytes a character takes in UTF-8. The slope does not.

Russian and Arabic are the most compressed languages at every BPE size. A Cyrillic or Arabic letter is two bytes that always occur together, so a merge can store one letter before it stores any word-internal structure. That is a property of the encoding, not of fusional or non-concatenative morphology. German is fusional too, and it is the least compressed language at 50,000 (0.263), because German letters are almost all one byte.

Within the one-byte Latin scripts, morphology is visible and small. Finnish, the clearest agglutinative case, is a little better than Hungarian at every BPE size (0.405 to 0.251, against Hungarian 0.419 to 0.255), and both are a little better than German. A Finnish word is a stem plus a chain of recurring suffixes, and a merge can store one of those pieces. The gap between Finnish and German at 50,000 is 0.013. The gap between German and Russian is 0.101.

Vietnamese and Mandarin do not form an isolating group. Vietnamese is 0.209 at 50,000 BPE. Many of its letters carry a diacritic and therefore take two bytes, so it sits between the two-byte scripts and plain Latin. Mandarin is 0.241. A character is three bytes, and once those three bytes are one token the ratio is already near one third; two-character words pull it lower. Isolating morphology does not put either language at one token per byte.

Unigram tells the same story only once the vocabulary is large enough to hold the alphabet. The joint corpus has about 11,400 characters. Byte fallback needs 256 pieces plus an unknown token, so a vocabulary of 5,000 or 10,000 can keep the frequent characters and only a few hundred multi-character pieces. Those two models are nearly character tokenizers, which on this scale means about 0.64 tokens per byte for one-byte Latin (Finnish 0.639 at 5,000) and about 0.37 for three-byte Mandarin. The lines are almost flat there, including a tiny rise for German (0.633 to 0.636) and Russian (0.447 to 0.453), because the extra slots between 5,000 and 10,000 go to rare characters rather than to longer pieces. At 20,000 the full character inventory fits and several thousand slots become subwords, and the ratios drop at once (Finnish 0.637 to 0.350). At 50,000, unigram is close to BPE and still a bit worse on every language (Finnish 0.263 against 0.251, Mandarin 0.258 against 0.241).

BPE does not have that flat stretch. Its base vocabulary is the 256 bytes, so each new merge is a multi-byte piece from the first vocabulary size onward. That is why BPE is ahead at 5,000 and 10,000, and why its curves slope the whole way.

The comparison that the plot supports: tokens per byte ranks the languages by script encoding first. Two-byte letters (Russian, Arabic) compress the most, one-byte Latin (Finnish, Hungarian, German) the least, and Mandarin, at three bytes per character, lands in between. Agglutination shows up only inside the Latin group, where Finnish is slightly ahead of Hungarian and German. A larger vocabulary helps all seven; it does not close the gap that the number of bytes per character opens.
